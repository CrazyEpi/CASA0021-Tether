# Tether: Designing a Connected Cycling Companion for Motivation, Awareness and Safety

## 1. Introduction

Tether is a connected cycling companion consisting of a handlebar-mounted device and a mobile application. Rather than working only as a navigation tool, it is designed to support cycling through real-time ride awareness, goal tracking, social connection, and safety-oriented interaction.

The project emerged from the observation that cyclists often need to manage several things at once: monitoring progress, staying aware of routes, comparing activity with friends, and responding to urgent situations. Smartphones can provide many of these functions, but they are not always suitable during cycling, as repeatedly checking a phone can be distracting and unsafe. Tether therefore explores how a cycling product can become part of the riding environment itself, offering simple and low-distraction support through a connected device and app system.

This report outlines the problem addressed by the project, identifies its users and scenarios, places it in the context of Connected Environments research, and discusses the key features, limitations, and future directions of the prototype.

## 2. Problem Identification and Motivation

Cycling is not only a physical activity but also a connected experience shaped by movement, environment, information, and social interaction. The World Health Organization recognises cycling as an important form of active mobility and highlights the need for safer transport systems for cyclists (World Health Organization, 2026).

### Problem Statement

The main problem addressed by this project is that current cycling interfaces do not effectively combine **awareness, motivation, safety, and social connection** in a way that is well integrated into the riding experience. Smartphones are powerful, but they are not ideal as the main interface while cycling. Looking at a phone can interrupt concentration and reduce safety, especially when the rider only needs quick and simple information.

Many existing cycling products also focus mainly on navigation or performance tracking. These are useful features, but they often overlook the more social and emotional aspects of riding. Cyclists may want to share progress with friends, compare performance, or feel reassured that support is available if something goes wrong. This suggests an opportunity for a product that supports not only information delivery, but also connection and responsiveness.

### Target Users and Scenarios

Tether is aimed at everyday cyclists, especially:
- commuters who need quick ride information,
- recreational riders who want visual motivation,
- friends who want to compare progress,
- and riders who may need a more visible emergency communication system.

The main user scenarios are:
1. **Individual riding**, where the rider sets a distance goal and tracks progress during the ride.
2. **Shared riding**, where two riders compare progress in real time even when cycling separately.
3. **Emergency situations**, where one rider can send an alert directly from the device.

### Project Objectives

The main objectives of Tether were:
- to reduce dependence on the smartphone during cycling,
- to provide low-distraction ride feedback,
- to support motivation through visible goal progress,
- to strengthen social connection between riders,
- and to create a more immediate emergency alert mechanism.

In this sense, Tether was motivated by the question: how can a cycling product become not just a tool, but a connected companion?

## 3. Tether in the Context of Connected Environments

Tether fits within the field of Connected Environments because it combines hardware, software, real-time data exchange, and human interaction within an outdoor mobility setting. Connected Environments research examines how digital systems become embedded in everyday life, linking people, devices, and contexts in meaningful ways.

The project is influenced by ideas from ubiquitous computing. Weiser (1991) argued that technology becomes most effective when it is integrated into everyday environments rather than constantly demanding attention. Tether reflects this principle by moving key ride information away from the smartphone and into a dedicated cycling device mounted directly on the bicycle.

The project also relates to research on mobile, wearable, and IoT systems, which shows how connected technologies can support well-being, decision-making, and context-sensitive interaction (Lee et al., 2019). Tether applies this logic by linking ride data, device feedback, app interaction, and social communication into one system.

For this reason, Tether should be understood not simply as a cycling gadget, but as a small connected ecosystem involving the rider, the bicycle, the device, the app, and the rider’s social network.

## 4. Design Concept and Product Overview

Tether consists of two main elements: a physical handlebar-mounted device and a companion mobile application. The device provides immediate and glanceable information during cycling, while the app supports planning, tracking, and social interaction.

A key design decision was to divide responsibilities between hardware and software. The device is designed for clarity and low-distraction use during movement. The app supports richer interaction, including route planning, goal setting, ride history, and shared activity. This allows the system to remain simple on the bike while offering more detailed features off the bike.

Tether is also positioned differently from products that focus only on minimalist navigation. For example, Beeline Velo 2 is marketed primarily as a simple cycling navigation device. Tether instead expands the category by combining ride awareness with social progress sharing and emergency communication. Its design concept therefore focuses not only on efficiency, but also on connection and support.

### Core Features of Tether

One of the most important functions of Tether is its **alert feature**. If a rider falls, calling a friend by phone may not be reliable, especially if the phone is on silent mode or not immediately visible. With Tether, the rider can press the screen on the device to send an **SOS signal**. The paired rider’s device then lights up immediately, creating a direct and visible alert. The friend can then open the app to check the rider’s GPS location and go to help.

Another key function is **goal-based progress visualisation**. A rider can set a target, such as **10 km**, and the circular light ring on the device shows progress toward that target. As more distance is completed, more of the ring lights up. When the target is completed, the full ring is illuminated.

Tether also includes a **shared riding progress feature**. On the physical device, the circular display is currently designed to show the progress of **two riders only**. One half of the ring shows the user’s progress, and the other half shows a friend’s progress. This allows simple comparison while riding. In contrast, the app can display multiple riders, enabling larger group challenges and leaderboard-style interaction.

Together, these features show that Tether is not only a cycling display device, but a connected product that supports motivation, communication, and safety.

## 7. Limitations, Production Considerations and Future Improvements

Although Tether presents a clear concept, the current prototype still has several limitations.

First, **cost** may be a challenge. Because the product combines hardware, app support, connectivity, and alert functions, it may be more expensive than simpler cycling devices. Future development would need to consider component selection and manufacturing efficiency in order to improve affordability.

Second, the prototype is still **technically limited**. It currently depends on a smartphone for GPS positioning and cloud connectivity, so it is not yet fully independent. The 900 mAh battery is also relatively small, limiting battery life for longer rides.

Third, the hardware still needs to be more **durable** for real cycling environments. Reliability may decrease in bad weather or rough outdoor conditions. The 3D-printed lampshade is slightly loose, and the casing seal still needs improvement, meaning waterproofing and structural stability are not yet fully resolved.

A further issue is **sustainability**. While rapid prototyping materials are useful during development, they are not necessarily ideal for long-term production. If developed further, the project should consider reducing material waste, improving repairability, and selecting more durable and potentially recyclable components.

Finally, the system has not yet been tested extensively with real users. More testing would help evaluate usability, performance, and feature relevance in real riding situations.

If given more time, Tether could be improved by reducing smartphone dependence, increasing battery capacity, strengthening the enclosure, improving waterproofing, refining the alert system, and expanding the app’s social features.

---

## 8. Conclusion

Tether was developed to address an important opportunity in cycling: creating a product that supports not only ride awareness, but also motivation, social connection, and safety.

By combining a physical device with a mobile app, Tether offers a broader vision of what a cycling product can be. Rather than acting only as a navigation tool, it functions as a connected companion that supports real-time feedback, emergency communication, and shared riding experiences.

Although the prototype still has practical and technical limitations, it provides a strong foundation for further development. With more refinement, testing, and attention to production and sustainability, Tether could become a more complete and reliable connected cycling product.
